Selection guide

Which drill bit for which rock?

Short answer: soft to medium rock runs with a flat head and ballistic buttons, hard and abrasive rock with a convex or concave head and spherical buttons, broken ground with a flat head and spherical buttons, and loose material needs a casing system rather than a bit alone.

Three steps to the equipment

Three inputs decide the selection: the rock you are in, the drilling method, and the connection on your rig.

Which head design suits which rock?

The convex head suits most drilling applications and is intended for hard and abrasive formations: it combines penetration rate with service life.

The flat (flatface) head is for general applications and is regarded as particularly effective in broken and soft formations.

The concave head is the all-round design for soft and hard formations alike. Its inward curve stabilises the bit in the hole and produces straighter holes.

Which carbide button shape should I choose?

Round, spherical carbide buttons are the robust choice for hard and abrasive formations and for deep holes; they can be used in every drilling application.

Ballistic buttons produce coarser cuttings and are ideal in less abrasive sedimentary deposits where penetration rate decides. In broken and hard formations they are less suitable.

Semi-ballistic buttons sit in between: faster than round ones, more wear-resistant than ballistic ones. In broken formations they too are less suitable.

DTH, rotary or overburden drilling?

In DTH drilling the hammer sits directly above the bit. It is the method for rock — in the 3″ to 6″ range the hammer and bit work as a matched pair, sized to your compressor's air flow and working pressure.

Rotary drilling works with thrust and rotation, without percussion. It is used where percussive energy is not needed or not wanted; the selection runs on hole diameter and depth.

In overburden drilling the casing advances with the bit. That is the route through loose material — sand, gravel, fill, slope debris — where the hole will not stand open without a tube.

Which thread connection fits my rig?

For top hammer drill steel the size decides: R32 small, T38 medium, T45 large, T51 heavy. The rig determines it.

For DTH in the 3″ to 6″ range, hammer and bit are ordered as a pair; the connecting thread is listed in the dimension tables for each hammer size.

If you are unsure about the connection: a photo of the thread end is enough for us to identify it.

What if the rock is unknown?

A photo of the cuttings is enough for us. Grain size, colour and fracture pattern say enough to narrow down head design and button shape.

What the rock means for the tool

Four rock classes cover most sites. What decides service life and progress in each is set out here, whether or not you work through the dialog on the home page.

Loose

Loose ground means the hole will not stand open.

Sand, gravel and slope debris collapse before the bit reaches depth. This calls for cased drilling — the casing belongs in the costing from the start, not as a variation order later.

Product group Casing systems and overburden strings with a ring bit and casing shoe.

What to watch Collapse in the annulus. Carbide wear is secondary here.

Soft to medium

Soft to medium: here it is the penetration rate that counts.

Sandstone, limestone and marl attack the carbide very little. Ballistic buttons play to their strength — as long as no quartz bands lie in between. As soon as the cuttings glitter, we talk again.

Product group Top hammer drill steel with a flat bit face, ballistic buttons.

What to watch Quartz content. It decides whether bit life keeps what the penetration rate promises.

Hard

In hard rock it is percussion energy that decides, not feed pressure.

Granite, gneiss and basalt are drilled by percussion. Raising the feed pressure loses buttons instead of gaining time. In jointed rock the bit face comes into it as well: concave keeps the hole straight.

Product group DTH equipment or heavy top hammer drill steel, spherical buttons.

What to watch Jointing. It pulls the hole off line long before the bit is worn out.

Abrasive

Quartz is 7 on the Mohs scale — wear sets the bit life.

In quartzite and quartz-bearing sandstone what counts is not the strength of the rock mass but what the cuttings do to the carbide. Spherical buttons and a flush that carries cleanly — if the cuttings stay in the hole, they grind the buttons away.

Product group Spherical buttons , more flushing holes, and shorter change intervals to plan for.

What to watch Flushing capacity. Here it is not a side issue but the lever.

Rock unknown

Unknown is the most common case — and no obstacle.

A photo of the cuttings or the profile of the neighbouring hole is usually enough for us to classify it. Go on to question 02; we settle the rock before anything is ordered.

What the three drilling methods cost and buy

DTH, rotary, overburden: what each is built for and where each runs out.

DTH

The hammer sits directly above the bit.

The energy is not lost along the drill string. First choice in hard rock and for straight holes at greater depth. What matters is that the compressor matches the hammer: air volume and working pressure, not just the rated output. We do that calculation for you before you order.

Sizes 3″ to 6″

Rotary

Turns with feed pressure instead of striking.

For soft to medium formations, for cleanly cased collars and everywhere vibration is a problem — on a slope, next to existing structures, close to buildings. Without percussion the attention shifts to feed and flushing.

Tools Rotary drilling tools , HDD tools and the matching flushing media.

What we check Feed pressure and torque of your rig against the formation.

Overburden drilling

The casing advances with the bit — the hole stays open.

Overburden drilling carries loose ground through to bedrock. That is the basis for anchors, pipe umbrellas and foundations on a slope. Ring bit, casing shoe and casing have to match as one string; parts bought separately rarely fit.

Tools Casing systems , pipe umbrellas, anchor systems.

What we check Whether string, feed beam and connection fit one another.

Tell us the depth, the hole diameter and what is meant to happen at the bottom of the hole — blast hole, anchor or foundation. The method then almost follows by itself. We propose two routes and give the penetration rate and the cost for both.

What the connection says about the rig

The thread size places the rig — and with it the range in which bit and drill string sensibly sit.

R32

R32 — the small connection with the least margin.

Anchor holes, slope drilling rigs, confined conditions. Because the joint is small it forgives the least: thread, feed beam and bit have to match exactly.

T38

T38 — the all-rounder in blast hole drilling.

Medium diameters, good availability of bits and extension rods. If you are looking for a standard in the range, this is usually it.

T45

T45 — for greater depths and diameters.

More percussion energy travels through the drill string. The joints need grease and checking, otherwise the wear moves from the tool into the threaded connection.

T51

T51 — the heavy connection.

Bench drilling rigs and large blast holes. Bits and rods in this size are partly made to order with a lead time; tell us early what you need and it will be ready in time.

DTH 3″–6″

DTH: not top hammer drill steel but a pair.

Hammer size, bit shank and hole diameter belong together; the drill string follows the hammer size. Tell us the size in inches and we check bit, hammer and air demand in one go.

Connection unknown

Connection unknown? A photo is enough.

We identify the connection reliably from the threaded end — and tell you straight away which bits for it are in stock. An article number is enough as well.
